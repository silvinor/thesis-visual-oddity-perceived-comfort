Abstract

Most people take in an everyday scene at a glance, because the brain is always predicting what it is about to see. Research has shown that scenes which break these predictions are harder to recognise, but much less is known about how they make people feel. Drawing on Schreuder et al.'s (2016) distinction between how an environment is read (external assessment) and how it feels (internal assessment), this study tested whether out-of-place elements make everyday scenes feel more uncomfortable, and whether that discomfort comes from how unexpected the scene looks. Eighty-four adults took part online, rating 32 images drawn from 16 everyday settings, such as a pub, a café and a beach. Each setting was edited into four versions: with or without an out-of-place element, such as a pelican in a café, and quiet or busy. For each image, participants rated how unexpected it looked and how comfortable they would feel entering it. Scenes with an out-of-place element were rated as less comfortable, in both quiet and busy versions. Busier scenes also added some discomfort, but through a separate route, as on average they did not look any more unexpected. The effect of oddity on discomfort ran through how unexpected the scene looked. Once that unexpectedness was taken into account, odd scenes felt slightly more comfortable than normal ones, a pattern that was not predicted and needs further testing. The findings suggest that discomfort with what looks out of place depends less on the thing itself than on the observer's expectation. Because expectations are learned, this discomfort can change, which points to education and forewarning, rather than changing what is different, as ways to reduce it.
