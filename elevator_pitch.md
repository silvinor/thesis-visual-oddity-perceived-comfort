## Opening

Picture walking into a café. A couple of tables, the smell of coffee. Except there's a pelican sitting at the table next to yours. Nothing about that pelican is dangerous, but most of us would feel a bit uneasy. I wanted to know why.

## Background

I read a paper by Greene, who theorised that we read scenes incredibly fast, because the brain is always guessing what it's about to see and checking those guesses against what's actually there. When a scene breaks those guesses, it costs us - odd scenes take longer to make sense of, and people describe them less accurately. But that research has all been about how well we *see* odd scenes. Hardly anyone had asked how they make us *feel*.

Which got me thinking ... I wonder if we subconsciously size up a place, judge it for normality, and then react to it with a "sense" of "comfortable here". Or, does oddity make one more uncomfortable?

## Predictions

At the start of this I made three predictions. One, odd scenes would feel more uncomfortable. Two, that how busy a scene was wouldn't matter relative to oddity. And lastly, the main and third one: odd scenes would feel more unexpected, and that's what would drive the discomfort ... not the oddity per se.

## What I did

So to test this I designed a randomised visual scene with both normal and oddities, and also noisy and not-noisy, in everyday settings. I specifically chose non-threatening scenes and made sure that only the oddity or the busyness was the variable.  I did this by selecting 16 static photos, and populating them with the help of AI image generation, the four variables of odd, not odd, quiet and noisy. For example a pub might have one bartender and a patron, or that bartender serving a single sheep, and then with a crowd of people, and again with a flock of sheep.

We then ran this experiment as an online survey, with eighty-four people from the US, UK and Australia - each rated thirty-two scenes online, on two questions: how expected does this feel, and would they feel comfortable stepping into it?

## What I found

The results were interesting in two ways. As expected, oddity mattered, but unexpectedly, busyness also mattered in the context of oddity, but less so, i.e. more busy the scene was with odd items, the more uncomfortable it was.

Also, as expected, oddity led predictably to discomfort when experienced from unexpectedness.Unexpectedness carried all of the effect. In fact, the route through unexpectedness turned out bigger than oddity's whole effect.

Which, to me is so interesting - because it suggests that once you take the surprise out, oddity actually felt slightly *more* comfortable. Maybe oddity without surprise is just interesting or curious - and I hadn't predicted that, so hadn't desiged the experiment to test that.

## What it means

So it comes down to this: the discomfort doesn't really live in the oddity. It lives in the gap between what you expected and what you got. That backs up the idea that the brain's predictions carry feelings, not just perceptions, and as far as I can tell it's the first time that framework has been tested with pictures alone.

The practical implications are interesting too; if you warn people about something unusual before they see it, that should shrink the surprise, and the discomfort with it.

Obviously the experiment had limits; people imagined these scenes rather than being in them, and I had picked the oddities myself. So a next time would expand on the images, and look to include more people, and in real scenarios.

## Short version

So, the short TL;DR is this: things that look out of place feel uncomfortable mostly because they're unexpected. And since expectations are learned, that discomfort can change. Which puts at least some of the responsibility on the person doing the looking, or the people that can teach, not just on what's being looked at.

Anyway, that's the gist of it.
