# Discussion

This study asked whether everyday scenes with something out of place feel more uncomfortable than the same scenes without it. It also asked whether that discomfort comes from how unexpected the scene looks. Based on Schreuder et al.'s (2016) idea of external and internal assessment, three outcomes were predicted. First, oddity would raise discomfort (H1). Second, how busy a scene was would not raise discomfort or change the effect of oddity (H2). Third, oddity would make scenes look more unexpected, and that unexpectedness would carry its effect on discomfort (H3).

Two of the three predictions were supported. Scenes with an out-of-place element were rated as less comfortable to enter than the same scenes without one. This held in both quiet and busy versions, and oddity was by far the strongest influence on discomfort, so H1 was supported. H2 was not supported. Busier scenes were rated a little less comfortable, even when nothing in them was odd, and oddity had a slightly larger effect in busy scenes than in quiet ones. Both of these effects were small next to the effect of oddity itself. H3 was supported. Oddity made scenes look far more unexpected, and the path through unexpectedness accounted for oddity's effect on discomfort. This held whether ratings were analysed one by one or averaged for each participant and condition. In fact, the path through unexpectedness was larger than oddity's overall effect. This is because, once unexpectedness was held constant, odd scenes were rated slightly *more* comfortable than normal ones. This opposite, direct effect was clear when single ratings were analysed, but it could not be told apart from zero once ratings were averaged.

Taken together, the results suggest that what makes an out-of-place element uncomfortable is less the element itself and more the expectation it breaks. The sections below first look at what this adds to research that has so far measured the perceptual costs of oddity, and what the mediation means for affective accounts of prediction. They then look at why busyness behaved differently from oddity, and what to make of the unexpected direct effect. The chapter ends with the study's limitations, future directions and implications.

## Oddity Carries an Affective Cost as Well as a Perceptual One

Oddity has a cost for how a scene feels, not just for how it is seen. The studies reviewed in the Introduction measured what a broken expectation does to recognition. Improbable scenes were understood more slowly and less accurately (Greene et al., 2015), and the mismatch showed up in brain signals (McLean et al., 2023; Ruzzoli et al., 2020). But those studies did not ask how the scene made people feel. Here, the out-of-place elements were clearly noticed, with oddity scenes rated as far more unexpected than normal ones. They also made the scenes less comfortable to imagine entering. So the kind of mismatch known to disrupt recognition also changes how a person feels about a scene.

Whether this cost is seen as perceptual or affective changes what it means. From a perceptual view, an odd scene is a problem for the visual system to solve. The cost is the time and accuracy it takes to solve it, and it should fade once the scene makes sense. From an affective view (Barrett & Bar, 2009), the mismatch is felt the moment it is noticed. The cost then falls on how comfortable the person feels, and on whether they would want to enter the scene at all. One feature of this study speaks to this, if only indirectly. Participants had no time limit and could see exactly what the odd element was, so they had most likely made sense of the scene before they answered. Even so, discomfort stayed high. This suggests that the discomfort is not just a side effect of the brain still working the scene out, and that it may remain once the scene has been understood.

This also invites a second look at the earlier perceptual findings. Things that are easy to process tend to feel pleasant, and things that are hard to process tend to feel a little unpleasant (Reber et al., 2004). So the slower, less accurate processing that Greene et al. (2015) found may also have carried a feeling that their measures were not built to pick up. The reverse may also be true here. Some of the discomfort participants reported may come from how hard the scene was to make sense of, rather than from what was in it. The perceptual and affective research may therefore be looking at two sides of the same process, which is what Barrett and Bar (2009) argued in theory. The present results fit that view, but because processing itself was not measured, they cannot test it directly.

The findings also parallel a pattern Schreuder et al. (2016) found in research using more than one sense. In that work, mismatched combinations of sight, sound and smell tended to make people feel worse. Here, a mismatch in meaning within a single image was enough to push feelings in the same negative direction. So the pattern does not need a mismatch between the senses. But this study had no sound or smell conditions, so it cannot say whether a mismatch between the senses adds to the effect or works the same way. It shows only that Schreuder et al.'s path from how a place looks to how it feels can work on the meaning of a scene, using sight alone.

## Discomfort Follows Expectation, Not the Thing Itself

The mediation result places the source of the discomfort in the observer's expectation, not in the odd element. Oddity made scenes look more unexpected, unexpectedness went along with discomfort, and the path through unexpectedness accounted for all of oddity's effect on discomfort, and more. This is the solid path shown in Figure 1. It is also what Barrett and Bar's (2009) account would lead one to expect. If the brain's predictions carry a feeling from the start, discomfort should follow how far a scene departs from what was predicted, not what the thing itself is. The result also fits the idea, set out in the Introduction, that oddity depends on context (Bar, 2004; Oliva & Torralba, 2007). A pelican is not uncomfortable in itself. A pelican on a jetty would barely be noticed. What participants reacted to was the gap between the scene they expected and the scene they were shown.

The finding that the path through unexpectedness was larger than oddity's total effect matters beyond this study. When two paths push in opposite directions, they partly cancel out, so the total effect hides how big each one is (MacKinnon et al., 2000). Here, the overall difference in discomfort between odd and normal scenes was what was left of a large rise driven by unexpectedness, minus a small pull the other way. A study that only measured oddity's overall effect would have found a moderate effect. It would have missed how much work unexpectedness was doing. For the same reason, research on oddity and feelings that reports only total effects may underestimate how much of the response comes from expectation.

These conclusions assume that unexpectedness shapes discomfort, rather than just going along with it. Because unexpectedness was measured and not manipulated, this study cannot fully test that assumption. This is taken up under the limitations below.

## Busyness Works Through a Separate Route

Activity level, or how busy a scene was, was meant to be a control, but it turned out to matter in its own right. The Introduction treated it as something to hold constant, so that any effect of oddity could not be put down to crowding. Busier scenes were rated less comfortable even when nothing in them was odd. This fits environmental psychology research linking crowding, and busy surroundings in general, to higher arousal and lower comfort (Mavros et al., 2022; Mehrabian & Russell, 1974). In that sense, H2 failing is less a surprise than a reminder that discomfort has more than one source.

Activity also raised discomfort in a different way from oddity. On average, busy scenes were not rated as more unexpected than quiet ones. So busyness made scenes less comfortable without making them look any more unexpected. This changes how the framework should be read. Schreuder et al. (2016) describe how an observer's reading of a place (external assessment) feeds into how that place feels (internal assessment), and for oddity, that is what happened here. But busyness shows that a feeling can also come straight from what is in a scene. A crowded platform is uncomfortable because it is crowded, not because it is surprising. How a scene looks is one input into how it feels, but it is not the only one.

This difference also tells us something about the two rating questions. If both questions had measured the same overall impression, anything that changed one answer should have changed the other. On average, busyness changed the discomfort answer but not the unexpectedness answer. Normal scenes show this most clearly (Tables 1 and 2). Busy normal scenes were rated slightly less unexpected than quiet ones, but more uncomfortable, so the two answers moved in opposite directions. This suggests that participants were telling apart how expected a scene looked from how it would feel to be in it, which is what the framework assumes.

The interaction between oddity and activity raises a related question: what counts as an oddity? In most scene families, the busy oddity version was made busy by adding more odd things, rather than more of what normally belongs there, such as people. A single sheep in the corner of the pub became a bar full of sheep and goats. One grand piano on the sand became a beach crowded with pianos, fridges and couches, and some of these scenes had no people at all. So in oddity scenes, "busy" mostly meant "more oddity". The larger effect of oddity in busy scenes may therefore come from the amount of oddity, not from oddity and crowding working together. There is another possible explanation. Mehrabian and Russell (1974) saw both novelty and complexity as adding to the overall load a place puts on a person, so oddity and busyness may simply add up. Either way, the interaction suggests that oddity is better thought of as a matter of degree than as simply there or not there.

The unexpectedness ratings across the four conditions (Table 1) fit this view. Busy oddity scenes were rated slightly more unexpected than quiet oddity scenes. But, as noted above, busy normal scenes were rated slightly *less* unexpected than quiet normal scenes. One possible reason is that a pub or platform full of people is the more normal version of that place. Adding people made normal scenes closer to what people expect, while adding more odd elements made oddity scenes further from it. Two small shifts in opposite directions would cancel out, which would explain why busyness had almost no overall effect on unexpectedness. This pattern was not predicted and was not formally tested. It is offered as something worth considering, not as a finding. Even so, it fits the idea that oddity depends on how an element relates to its setting.

## An Unpredicted Direct Effect

The negative direct effect was the least expected result. When unexpectedness was held constant, oddity scenes were rated slightly more comfortable than normal ones. This is the opposite of what a simple "odd is unpleasant" view would predict. One possible reason, thought of only after the result was seen, is that an odd element that does not surprise may feel interesting rather than unsettling. Research on interest suggests that people find things interesting when they are new but can still be understood (Silvia, 2005). Accounts of art based on prediction error also suggest that a mismatch which can be resolved may feel mildly rewarding (Van de Cruys & Wagemans, 2011). A harmless pelican in a café, once accepted, is arguably funnier than it is threatening. If so, the same element has two opposite effects: discomfort through surprise, and a small lift through novelty. That would help explain why harmless oddity raised discomfort only moderately, even though it raised unexpectedness so sharply.

But other explanations predict the same result. Oddity and unexpectedness moved together so closely that there was little separate variation left to estimate the direct path, which makes that estimate sensitive to small quirks in the data. After rating a scene as very unexpected, participants may also have toned down their discomfort rating by contrast. Or an odd element might make an otherwise unclear scene easier to read. This study did not measure curiosity or interest, so it cannot tell these explanations apart. The direct effect also could not be told apart from zero once ratings were averaged for each participant and condition. Presenting an explanation thought of after the fact as if it had been predicted is one of the research choices known to produce false-positive findings (Wicherts et al., 2016). So the direct effect is reported as a pattern worth testing, not a finding to be explained, and the interest explanation is set aside rather than claimed.

## Limitations

The first limitation concerns the step from unexpectedness to discomfort. Unexpectedness was measured, not manipulated. It was also rated first, on the same screen as discomfort. So while oddity was manipulated experimentally, the link between unexpectedness and discomfort is correlational. At least four readings fit the data, and each leads to a different conclusion. First, unexpectedness may cause discomfort, as predicted, in which case the study has found a mechanism. Second, a single overall sense that the scene is "wrong" may drive both ratings. The two questions would then partly measure the same judgement, and the mediation would describe how that judgement was expressed rather than how one process feeds another. Third, rating a scene as unexpected may have nudged participants to rate it as uncomfortable, so part of the link would come from question order. Fourth, the cause may run the other way, with a scene that feels uneasy being judged as less expected. The way busyness moved one rating but not the other argues against the second reading, but it cannot rule out the others. What can be said with confidence is that discomfort closely tracks unexpectedness, and that unexpectedness statistically accounts for oddity's effect. That unexpectedness *causes* the discomfort is a reasonable idea, but it has not been tested (Spencer et al., 2005).

A second limitation is the oddities themselves. Every out-of-place element was chosen by one person, and all were harmless objects and animals. They were kept harmless by design, as set out in the Method, and the full image set was reviewed and approved by the ethics panel. Participants agreed that these elements were out of place. But agreement on obvious cases says little about the wider range of things people find odd. The results therefore describe one kind of oddity: obvious, harmless and non-social. Threatening oddity would probably add discomfort through a route of its own (Damiano et al., 2021), so the finding that discomfort runs mostly through unexpectedness may not hold for it. Subtle oddity might raise unexpectedness only a little, and may fall where the interest effect described above becomes relatively stronger. Social oddity, which is the case behind the study's wider implications, was not tested at all. Because oddity and unexpectedness moved together so closely, the design had limited power to separate the direct path from the indirect one. This is part of why the direct effect proved fragile.

A third limitation is statistical. The main analyses pooled all ratings and treated each one as independent. But ratings from the same person tend to be similar, and every scene family was seen twice. Because the design was balanced, this affects how precise the estimates are, not the estimates themselves. The effect of oddity on discomfort was large enough that this is unlikely to threaten H1. The interaction between oddity and activity was the smallest effect in the model, and its confidence interval came close to zero, so understated standard errors could change its result. When the mediation was re-run on each participant's average for each condition, the indirect path still held. The direct effect's confidence interval then included zero. This reflects the precision lost by averaging, not evidence that unexpectedness carried the whole effect. Seeing each scene family twice may also have made scenes seem less unexpected the second time. But that would weaken the link from oddity to unexpectedness, so it would work against H3, not in its favour.

The measures and setting add further limits. Both questions were written for this study. As single items, their internal consistency could not be checked. Single items can work well for narrow, concrete judgements like these (Allen et al., 2022), but their validity here rests on the framework rather than on psychometric evidence. The discomfort question also asked participants to imagine entering the scene. So the study measured expected discomfort, not discomfort actually felt, and people standing in an odd place might feel more or less uncomfortable than they imagined.

The instructions told participants that the images had been altered with AI. This may have drawn their attention to the altered elements, and invited them to answer the way they thought the study wanted (Orne, 1962). Because every condition came with the same instructions, this alone could not create a difference between odd and normal scenes, but it may have made one larger. Finally, all participants lived in four Western, English-speaking countries. Expectations are learned, so what counts as out of place in a pub or on a subway platform is likely to differ between cultures. The scenes used here may not read as odd, or as normal, elsewhere.

None of these limitations undermines the main finding: oddity raised discomfort, and unexpectedness accounted for that effect. But they do set its limits. The study shows how obvious, harmless oddity in imagined everyday scenes relates to expected discomfort. It also shows that the path through unexpectedness is a statistical account, not yet a proven causal one.

## Future Directions

Several of these limitations point to clear next steps. The most direct would be to manipulate expectation instead of measuring it. Cueing studies such as McLean et al. (2023) offer a model. If participants were warned about an unusual element before seeing the scene, the account developed here predicts that those scenes would be rated less unexpected, and so less uncomfortable. One study like this would test the causal step from unexpectedness to discomfort, separate the two ratings in time, and test the practical implication discussed below.

Future image sets should also be rated beforehand by people who did not make them, and should cover a wider range of harmless oddity, from subtle to obvious. Busyness and the amount of oddity should be varied separately, so that a busy oddity scene has more of what normally belongs there, but not more odd elements. This would show whether the interaction found here comes from crowding, from the amount of oddity, or from both. Multilevel models that treat both participants and scene families as random factors would handle the nesting in the data, and would let the results apply beyond the particular scenes used (Judd et al., 2012).

The direct effect needs its own test. Adding a measure of interest or curiosity, alongside unexpectedness and discomfort, would show whether harmless oddity has a positive pull once surprise is taken into account. It would also show whether that pull changes with how subtle or obvious the oddity is. Measuring discomfort that is actually felt, not just imagined, would show whether the pattern holds in real settings. This could use physiological recordings like those in crowding research (Mavros et al., 2022), or the choices people make in immersive settings. Finally, using social images would take the first step from the non-social case tested here towards the social question raised by Sasson et al. (2017).

## Implications

The main theoretical implication is that the discomfort caused by an out-of-place element depends on the observer's expectation, not on the element. Oddity depends on context (Bar, 2004), and these results suggest that the feeling it causes does too. This is consistent with Barrett and Bar's (2009) view that perceptual and affective predictions are made together. It also brings Schreuder et al.'s (2016) path from how a place looks to how it feels to visual scenes alone. At the same time, it shows that how a place feels draws on more than how it looks, since busyness raised discomfort without changing how expected a scene seemed. In terms of method, the scene-family design offers a way to change how out of place a scene is while keeping the photograph's setting the same. The opposing paths found here also show why research in this area should look past total effects when paths may push in opposite directions.

The practical implication follows from this. If discomfort tracks unexpectedness, then reducing the surprise should reduce the discomfort, even if the unusual feature stays exactly as it is. Think of an unusual layout, an art installation, or a familiar building used in an unfamiliar way. Warning people about features like these ahead of time should make them seem less unexpected, and so less uncomfortable. This prediction follows from the findings but was not tested by them. The warning study described above would test it directly.

The same logic may extend, more tentatively, to how people respond to each other. Sasson et al. (2017) found that observers formed less favourable impressions of autistic people within seconds, from appearance alone. If the process found here also works in social perception, the thing to change is the observer's expectation, not the autistic person. Impressions of autistic adults were more favourable among observers who knew more about autism (Sasson & Morrison, 2019). They also depended more on the observers than on the autistic adults, and among observers with stigmatising attitudes, being told the diagnosis made them worse (Morrison et al., 2019). A label tells people what to expect, but not what autism is. Because this study used no social images, this link is a hypothesis to test, not an implication to assume. If it holds, the lever is education rather than disclosure alone, because expectedness is about understanding (Bar, 2004; Jones et al., 2021).

## Conclusion

What looks out of place feels uncomfortable mostly because it is unexpected. In this study, adding a harmless out-of-place element to an everyday scene made it less comfortable to imagine entering, and that effect ran almost entirely through how unexpected the scene looked. Busyness added discomfort in a separate way. And once surprise was taken into account, the oddity itself may even have pulled slightly the other way. By testing an affective account of prediction with visual scenes alone, the study suggests that the discomfort sits in the gap between what the observer expected and what they saw. Expectations are learned, so this discomfort can change. That moves at least some of the responsibility for it from the element to the observer. If the same holds for how people see each other, then the way to ease discomfort with people who seem different is not to change them, but to educate those doing the looking.
